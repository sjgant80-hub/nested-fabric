Twelve small minds, sharing what works, losing nodes gracefully.

Twelve settling-cell nodes in three clusters each improve on their own data. Every round they share their shapes inside their cluster, and every other round across clusters, through signed shares that each node tests on its own exam before taking. A rogue tries every round. Then nodes are cut and links dropped, and what is left is judged on handwriting no node trained on.

What this is

This is Rung 6 of a ladder of tests toward computing by geometry (small networks that read by settling into stored patterns, with no trained model) instead of by large models. Rung 4 showed one node can safely improve another. Rung 6 asks what happens with many: does a fabric of small nodes, sharing locally and widely, beat the same nodes working alone, and does it keep working when nodes die and messages are lost?

Words on this page

NodeOne small program that reads handwritten digits. Here there are twelve, each with its own digits to learn from.
ShapeWhat a node reads with: two hundred stored digit patterns, plus two numbers that set how widely each pattern pulls. About 13 KB of text.
SettleHow a node reads a digit: it flips the digit's bits one at a time towards its stored patterns until nothing more changes. The class of the patterns it ends up nearest is the answer.
ImproveCode proposes a small change to the shape and keeps it only if it wins on a fresh batch of the node's own digits. No model is trained (Rung 2).
Share, signedA node sends its shape to another, signed with its own Ed25519 key, so the receiver can check who sent it and that not one byte changed.
RosterThe list of keys a node will listen to: the twelve nodes.
ExamBefore taking a share, the receiver reads a fresh batch of its own digits with both shapes, and takes the new one only if it gets at least two more of them right.
RogueA thirteenth sender that signs its messages properly but is on no roster. Only the roster stops it.
Cluster, gatewayFour nodes that share with each other every round; the lowest-numbered live one is the cluster's gateway, which shares with the other clusters every second round.
SealCommitting the claims (seal 1), then the frozen results (seal 2), to the public record before the test digits are read, so nothing can be tuned after the fact.
Mutation gateA tool (witness) that breaks the code in small ways, one at a time, and checks the tests catch every break. Clean means every one was caught.

How it works

  1. Improve. Each round, every live node runs Rung 2's model-free loop for 15 cycles on its own 2,500 training digits.
  2. Share locally. Each node signs its shape and sends it to its cluster peers.
  3. Share widely. In rounds 2 and 4, each cluster's gateway sends its shape to the other gateways.
  4. Check before taking. Every share goes through Rung 4's receive: key on the roster, signature on the raw bytes, a cell that builds, and a win on the receiver's own fresh exam.
  5. Lose things. In n25 and n50, 3 or 6 nodes go silent after round 1; in l30, each message is dropped with 30% chance.

Blueprint

    Adoption

    For a business

    Many small sites or devices can learn from each other without a centre, and keep working when some go dark. Each still decides for itself what to take.

    For engineering

    Local-first gossip with signed shares and a local acceptance test; seeded loss and drops; every event in a log that re-derives byte for byte.

    For risk and procurement

    A sender on no roster is refused before its bytes are parsed; a lost node can neither send nor receive. In this simulation nothing is sent outside the twelve nodes; a real deployment needs its own network design.

    A pilot, step by step

    1. Pick one task every site does, with a right answer you can check later.
    2. Write down the bars it must meet before anything is built, and seal them.
    3. Give each site a key (generated on the site and kept secret: the keys on this page are derived from public seeds only so that anyone can re-run the record), and put every site's key on one roster.
    4. Run the fabric on each site's past data, offline, with the losses you expect: sites down, links dropping.
    5. Freeze it, then judge it once on data it never saw. The fee is due only if it beat your current way.
    6. Then run it beside your current process, deciding nothing, before it is trusted with anything.

    What it runs on: Node.js 20 or a current browser. No GPU, no cloud service, no model download.

    White paper: a fabric of settling cells

    The question. Does sharing proven shapes across a fabric of small model-free nodes beat isolation, and how much does it lose when nodes and links fail?

    The method. 12 nodes on disjoint MNIST training shards, 4 rounds of local improvement and sharing, five sealed scenarios, every share through Rung 4's receive. The final shapes were frozen by hash; then every surviving node read the first 2,000 test digits once.

    The results, from the record:

      The limits, plainly:

      • A simulation in one process, not a deployment over real networks.
      • The test digits were read before by Rungs 3–5, for other claims.
      • The nodes are weak: each improved for only 60 cycles from Rung 1's cell. The claims are about what sharing adds and what survives loss, not about how well a node reads.
      • A share replaces a node's whole shape; nodes never merge two shapes. Not on a chip (that is Rung 7).
